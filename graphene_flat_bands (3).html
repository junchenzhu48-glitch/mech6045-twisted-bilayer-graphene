Twisted bilayer graphene

A single graphene sheet has a honeycomb lattice and a conical energy spectrum near K. Rotate a second sheet by a small angle and the two atomic patterns produce a much larger moiré period. Near a first magic angle of about 1.1°, interlayer tunnelling can greatly reduce the dispersion of the central electronic bands. [1]

Research, applications and outlook

What does a real flat band look like?

In 2026, Xiao and colleagues mapped electronic energies and momenta using a quantum twisting microscope. Near the magic angle the bands were very flat over much of the mini-zone, yet dispersed more strongly near its centre, Γ. Their measurements show where interactions reshape the simple picture below. [2]

A circuit in one graphene bilayer

Rodan-Legrain and colleagues used local gate voltages to define a weak link between two superconducting regions in the same bilayer. A supercurrent crossed the link, forming a tunable Josephson junction. A double-barrier gate configuration also let the device operate as a single-electron transistor. [3]

What could come next?

Gate-defined junctions offer a route to reconfigurable superconducting circuits. For a proposed gate-tunable qubit, a further test would be whether it preserves a quantum state while its gate voltage is adjusted. The demonstrated junction is a building block for that research. [3]

How to read the bands · model, parameters and reproducible calculation

What flattens? Follow each colored line from K through Γ and M and back to K. The readouts give max(E) − min(E) for each central band on these 58 sampled momenta. A full two-dimensional bandwidth requires sampling the interior of the mini-zone as well. E = 0 is the midpoint of the two central eigenvalues at K for each twist.

Why does twist matter? The separation of the two layers’ Dirac points is kθ = 2K sin(θ/2), K = 4π/(3a). Decreasing θ raises the dimensionless coupling wAB/(ℏvFkθ). Hybridization changes the dispersion, with a narrow central pair near the first magic angle. The first magic angle is approximately 1.1°, model- and sample-dependent. The 1.05–1.12° highlight is a presentation guide.

Calculation. Single valley, single spin, noninteracting Bistritzer–MacDonald continuum Hamiltonian with three tunnelling harmonics. ℏvF = 600 meV·nm, wAA = 80 meV, wAB = 110 meV. Each layer has 61 reciprocal-lattice vectors (four hexagonal shells), giving a 244 × 244 Hermitian matrix. Each 0.01° step from 0.50° to 2.60° has its own diagonalization. Lines join neighboring k samples in proportion to momentum distance. Stored energies are rounded to 0.02 meV.

Scope. The 44 nm map shows the geometric registry of rigid sheets (atomic zoom spans 3.2 nm). Unequal tunnelling amplitudes phenomenologically capture one effect of relaxation. Strain, explicit atomic displacements and electron interactions are omitted. The spectrum alone cannot establish superconductivity. Below 0.50° the map remains available.

How the moiré period changes

As twist angle increases, the moiré period decreases.

The geometry gives about 12.81 nm at 1.10°.

What happens when you halve the angle?

From 2° to 1°, the moiré period grows from about 7.05 nm to 14.09 nm. For small angles in radians, LM ≈ a/θ.
